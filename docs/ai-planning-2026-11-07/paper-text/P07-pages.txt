Abstract
To address the issues of uneven grayscale distribution, weak defect features, and small
target scales on the coating surface of LCD polarizers during manufacturing, an improved
YOLOv10-N-based method is proposed for surface defect detection. First, a polarizer
coating defect dataset is constructed based on the LCD polarizer coating process and
the characteristics of coating defects. Adaptive median filtering is then employed for
image denoising, while a particle-swarm-optimization-based improved histogram equal-
ization method is adopted for image enhancement. Next, the Scale-aware Pyramid Pooling
(SCPP) module is introduced into the C2f module of the backbone network to construct
the C2f_SCPP feature extraction module, thereby improving the model’s ability to detect
coating defects with different morphologies through multi-scale semantic feature fusion. In
addition, rotation-equivariant convolution PreCM is incorporated into the SPPF module of
the backbone network to build the SPPF_PreCM module, which effectively suppresses fea-
ture redundancy and scale conflicts while strengthening the representation of tiny defects.
Finally, while retaining the original Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) branch of YOLOv10,
WIoU is used to replace CIoU as the IoU loss term in bounding box regression, thereby
improving localization accuracy and accelerating model convergence during training. Ex-
perimental results show that, compared with YOLOv10-N, the proposed method improves
mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 1.8 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, demonstrating
its effectiveness for polarizer coating defect detection. However, its generalization capabil-
ity under diverse production environments, varying illumination conditions, and complex
noise scenarios still requires further investigation.
Keywords: polarizer surface; defect detection; feature extraction; YOLOv10; cross-scale
feature fusion

devices have witnessed sustained growth in market demand [1]. In recent years, China’s
display panel industry chain has been continuously improved, forming one of the world’s
most important production and supply bases. Relevant research reports indicate that

China’s LCD panel production accounted for approximately 78% of the global total in 2025,
with the industrial scale and international influence continuously rising [2]. However, dur-

are easily affected by processing, transportation, storage and other factors. Surface defects
such as scratches, cracks, white spots, light leakage, damage and film rupture frequently
occur, resulting in degraded product performance and reduced yield, which has become
a pressing issue in the industry. Therefore, researching and developing high-precision
and high-efficiency detection technologies for polarizer coating defects has become a key
direction for LCD manufacturers to improve product quality and production efficiency.
In the early stage, the detection of coating defects on polarizers mainly relied on
manual visual inspection, which was susceptible to human factors, leading to missed
detection and false detection. Such methods could not meet the requirements of contin-
uous optimization and upgrading of polarizer coating production lines. To improve the
detection performance of polarizer coating defects, many scholars have gradually applied
machine vision-based detection methods to production lines. Defect detection is real-
ized by extracting artificial features such as threshold segmentation [3], edge contours [4],
and texture statistics [5], combined with classifiers such as support vector machines and
random forests, which have improved the detection efficiency to a certain extent [6].
Jian et al. [7] proposed an improved MPSG algorithm for defect recognition and seg-
mentation detection by introducing a multi-scale feature fusion mechanism. Ho et al. [8]
developed an automatic optical inspection system to detect the cross-sectional profile and
surface of cut thin-film-transistor liquid-crystal display panels. However, such methods
still rely on feature engineering in essence. When dealing with weak defects on polarizers,
it is difficult to balance missed detection and false alarms simultaneously. Against this
background, deep learning methods can adaptively extract multi-scale defect features from
a large number of samples with their end-to-end automatic feature learning capability.
Tien et al. [9] proposed an effective method for micro-defect detection and classification of
optical films based on improved YOLOv7.Joseph Redmon et al. [10] proposed the YOLO
series, which performs object localization and classification simultaneously using a sin-
gle neural network, greatly improving the detection speed. Wang et al. [11] proposed a
method for detecting and identifying LBE coating defects based on an improved binary
tree support vector machine (BT-SVM). Sheng [12] proposed an improved algorithm for
inkjet character detection on polarizer surfaces based on YOLOv8-N. DCNv3 was used to
replace the standard convolution in the C2f module of the backbone network, and the EMA
attention mechanism was incorporated to construct the DEC2f feature extraction module.
Liu et al. [13] proposed a lightweight neural network based on deep learning, which meets
the requirements of accuracy and real-time performance for industrial polarizer defect
detection. The model achieved an accuracy of 93.4% on the test set. Xia et al. [14] proposed
an improved Faster-RCNN-based network for polarizer surface defect detection to improve
the detection ability for small defects.
Although existing algorithms for polarizer coating defect detection have achieved
certain performance improvements, they remain vulnerable to challenges such as low
grayscale contrast and blurred defect boundaries. Consequently, the detection accuracy
for low-contrast defects, such as scratches, white spots, and cracks, still needs to be further
improved. To address these issues, this paper proposes an improved YOLOv10-N-based
method for surface defect detection in liquid crystal display polarizer coating manufac-
turing. The proposed approach is developed from three perspectives, namely image
preprocessing, enhanced feature extraction, and optimized bounding box regression.

2.1. Polarizer Coating Image Acquisition and Preprocessing
2.1.1. Polarizer Coating Process Analysis and Image Acquisition
As one of the key functional components of liquid crystal displays (LCDs), the polar-
izer is produced at the front end of the display module manufacturing process and has a
direct impact on subsequent lamination and display quality. The production process of the
LCD polarizer is shown in Figure 1. This figure was drawn using Microsoft Visio 2019. The
general procedure is as follows: optical functional materials are formulated into a coating
solution, which is uniformly applied onto the surface of the base film through precision
coating. The film then enters the drying stage to remove solvents and volatile components,
followed by the curing section to complete cross-linking, solidification, and film formation.
Finally, the finished film is wound up into a continuous roll [15].

Therefore, improving the accuracy of polarizer coating defect detection and recognition

Combined with the actual production scenario of polarizer coating, the image ac-
quisition and dataset construction of polarizer coating surface defects are realized by the
automatic optical acquisition system shown in Figure 4. The system is mainly composed
of a camera imaging unit, lighting equipment, transmission system, photoelectric sensor,
industrial computer, and programmable logic controller (PLC). The camera continuously
images the polarizer coating surface under stable illumination provided by the lighting
equipment and transmits the collected image data to the industrial computer in real time
for preprocessing, such as caching, cropping, and labeling to form raw samples for dataset
construction. At the same time, the photoelectric sensor provides a trigger signal to the PLC,
and the PLC outputs Trigger/Strobe to realize synchronous control of camera exposure
and light flashing, so as to ensure image clarity and consistency under high-speed move-
ment. Subsequently, the industrial computer performs position association and traceability
management on the samples according to the corresponding timestamps or position codes
of the images. After image quality screening, samples containing typical defects such as
huahen, liewen, baidian, louguang, bengque, pomo, and wuzi are classified and annotated.
Finally, the polarizer coating surface defect image dataset is constructed, providing data
support for the subsequent training and evaluation of defect detection algorithms.

2.2. Polarizer Coating Defect Detection
To improve the accuracy and efficiency of coating defect detection in liquid crystal
display devices, this study proposes a surface defect detection method for polarizer coating
manufacturing based on an improved YOLOv10-N architecture. Specifically, the C2f_SCPP
feature extraction module is constructed by incorporating the Scale-Aware Pyramid Pooling
(SCPP) module, the rotation-equivariant convolution module PreCM is embedded into
the backbone SPPF module, and WIoU is adopted to replace CIoU as the loss term for
bounding box regression. The overall framework is shown in Figure 5.
2.2.1. Network Structure of YOLOv10 Algorithm
YOLOv10 is a new-generation model developed on the basis of the YOLO series of
one-stage object detection algorithms [20]. Compared with YOLOv8, while maintaining
real-time detection capability, YOLOv10 places more emphasis on end-to-end detection
and collaborative efficiency–accuracy design, and further advances toward an NMS-Free

YOLOv10 introduces spatial-channel decoupled downsampling, CIB, large-kernel

3. Results
3.1. Experimental Setup and Details
3.1.1. Experimental Platform and Software Environment
The experimental environment in this study comprised both a software develop-
ment environment and a model training environment. For software development, the
hardware used for both development and deployment was equipped with a 12th Gen
Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12400F processor (Intel Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA) running
at 2.50 GHz, with Windows 10 Professional as the operating system. The software tools
and development environment included Python 3.8.20, Qt 5.15.9, PyQt 5.15.2, and OpenCV
4.11.0. For model training, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 Ti GPU was employed, and the
deep learning environment consisted of CUDA 11.8, PyTorch 2.0.1 + cu118, Torchvision
0.15.2 + cu118, and Torchaudio 2.0.2 + cu118. The proposed detection model was imple-
mented within the Ultralytics framework and developed based on YOLOv10-N. To ensure
fairness and reproducibility, all comparative experiments were conducted under identical
hardware and software conditions.
3.1.2. Dataset Construction and Division
Considering the balance between preserving defect details and reducing computa-
tional cost, the original polarizer surface defect images with a resolution of 1500 × 1000
were uniformly cropped to 640 × 640 in this study. Based on defect types commonly ob-
served in actual production, seven categories, including scratches, cracks, white spots, light
leakage, damage, stains, and film rupture, were selected to construct a polarizer coating
surface defect dataset. The dataset comprises 4834 images in total. As multiple defect
targets may coexist in a single image, the total number of annotated instances across all
categories exceeds the total number of images. The distribution of samples in each defect
category is shown in Table 1. Since defects from different categories exhibit variations in
morphology and scale, both the number of images and the number of annotated instances
in each category were statistically analyzed to reflect the true distribution characteristics of
the dataset. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 70%,
15%, and 15%, respectively, corresponding to 3384, 725, and 725 images, respectively.

3.1.3. Training Parameter Settings
To ensure a fair comparison, all models were trained under the same parameter
settings. The input image size was set to 640 × 640, the batch size to 16, and the number
of training epochs to 200. The initial learning rate was set to 0.008, with the final learning
rate factor specified as 0.01. SGD was adopted as the optimizer, with a momentum of
0.937 and a weight decay of 0.0005. To improve training stability during the early stage, the
number of warm-up epochs was set to 5, while the warm-up momentum and warm-up
bias learning rate were set to 0.8 and 0.1, respectively.
Regarding the training strategy, pretrained weights were adopted for model initial-
ization, the random seed was fixed at 0, and deterministic training mode was enabled to
minimize the influence of random factors on the experimental results. Automatic mixed
precision training was disabled throughout the training process. The confidence threshold
and IoU threshold were set to 0.001 and 0.7, respectively, and the maximum number of
detected objects per image was set to 300. Except for the architectural modifications, the
baseline and improved models were trained under identical settings, including input size,
number of training epochs, learning rate parameters, and data augmentation strategies,
thereby ensuring the fairness and reproducibility of the experimental results.
For data augmentation, HSV color perturbation, random translation, random scaling,
random horizontal flipping, and Mosaic augmentation were applied during the training
https://doi.org/10.3390/coatings16040451

stage. The HSV parameters were set to 0.015, 0.7, and 0.4, respectively, while the translation
factor, scaling factor, horizontal flipping probability, and Mosaic augmentation probability
were set to 0.1, 0.5, 0.5, and 1.0, respectively. Mosaic augmentation was disabled in the
final 10 epochs of training. No data augmentation was applied to the validation or test set.
During the training process, model performance was evaluated on the validation set at

3.1.4. Evaluation Metrics and Model Selection Criteria
To comprehensively assess the detection performance of the model, Precision, Recall,
mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95 were adopted as the primary evaluation metrics in this
study. Precision measures the proportion of true positives among all samples predicted
as positive, whereas Recall indicates the proportion of actual defects that are correctly
identified by the model. mAP@0.5 refers to the mean average precision over all categories at
an IoU threshold of 0.5, while mAP@0.5:0.95 denotes the mean average precision computed
over IoU thresholds ranging from 0.5 to 0.95 with an interval of 0.05. Compared with
mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5:0.95 requires higher localization accuracy and therefore provides a
more comprehensive evaluation of the overall detection performance of the model.
In addition to detection accuracy metrics, the number of parameters (Parameters),
computational cost (GFLOPs), and inference speed (FPS) were further evaluated to compre-
hensively assess the model complexity and real-time detection capability, thereby analyzing
the trade-off between detection accuracy and deployment efficiency of the improved model.
For model selection, the performance of the model was evaluated on the validation set
after each training epoch, and the weights corresponding to the highest mAP@0.5:0.95 were
selected as the final model for subsequent testing. The same selection criterion was adopted
for both the baseline and improved models to ensure the fairness and comparability of the
experimental results.

with the subjective comparison results presented in Figure 13, adaptive median filtering
not only suppresses background noise effectively but also preserves defect edges and
texture details relatively well. It should be noted that PSNR serves primarily as an auxiliary
indicator for evaluating denoising methods from the perspective of image quality. In
practical industrial defect imaging scenarios, however, its explanatory power is limited

4. Discussion
In summary, the results of this study show that, in the current experimental setting,
collaborative optimization from image preprocessing, feature modeling, and bounding
box regression can improve polarizer coating surface defect detection performance. The
experimental results confirm that multi-scale feature fusion, direction-sensitive modeling,
and loss optimization all contribute positively to detection accuracy. From the broader
perspective of industrial vision inspection research, this work provides a useful reference
for the application of lightweight object detection models to industrial surface defect
detection. Nevertheless, the present study is based on a single dataset collected from one
practical production scenario, and therefore, the cross-scene generalization of the proposed
method has not been sufficiently verified. Future work will further investigate robustness
and generalization under different production environments, illumination conditions, and
complex noise scenarios.

(2)
By incorporating the scale-aware pyramid pooling module (SCPP) into the C2f mod-
ule, a C2f_SCPP feature extraction module was constructed to enhance the model’s
capability to represent defects with complex morphologies. Meanwhile, the rotation-
equivariant convolution module PreCM was embedded into the SPPF module to
build the SPPF_PreCM module, thereby strengthening the modeling of direction-
sensitive features and improving the detection of small defects. Experimental results
show that all the proposed model enhancement modules make positive contributions
to detection performance, thus further improving the model’s ability to recognize
coating-surface defects in polarizer films.
(3)
While preserving the original Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) branch in YOLOv10,
WIoU was introduced to replace CIoU as the IoU loss term for bounding box re-
gression, thereby improving the target localization process. Experimental results
demonstrate that the proposed method achieves satisfactory detection performance
on the polarizer coating-surface defect dataset under the current experimental con-
ditions. Specifically, compared with YOLOv10-N, the proposed method improves
mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 1.8 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, thus
improving overall detection performance while preserving model lightweightness.
